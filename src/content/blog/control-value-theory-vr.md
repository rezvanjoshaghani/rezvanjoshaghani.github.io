When we submitted our IEEE VR paper on XR-based glove hygiene training, one reviewer flagged our null self-efficacy finding as a limitation. But we argue it's actually one of the most interesting results in the study — and Control-Value Theory (CVT) explains why.

CVT, developed by Reinhard Pekrun, proposes that academic emotions arise from two appraisals: perceived control over an activity and the subjective value assigned to it. In our three-arm RCT, VR participants showed significantly reduced anticipatory anxiety compared to the multimedia control group. But their self-efficacy scores didn't budge. At first glance, this looks like VR only did half its job.

Through the CVT lens, the interpretation changes. Anxiety reduction without self-efficacy change suggests that VR shifted the value appraisal (the task felt less threatening) without altering the control appraisal (participants didn't feel more capable). This makes sense: a VR simulation can defamiliarize a scary procedure, reducing its perceived stakes, without necessarily teaching the motor skills that build confidence. The CAMIL framework for immersive learning supports this reading — presence and emotional engagement are separate mechanisms from skill acquisition.
