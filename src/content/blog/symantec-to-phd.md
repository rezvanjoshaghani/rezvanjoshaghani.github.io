When I left Symantec (by then rebranded under Broadcom), colleagues asked the obvious question: why leave a stable software engineering job for a PhD? The honest answer is that I'd been thinking about it for years but kept finding reasons to stay. The paycheck, the familiarity, the comfort of solving problems with known solutions. But there was a growing itch — a sense that I was optimizing within constraints someone else had set, rather than questioning the constraints themselves.

At Symantec I built enterprise security dashboards, managed reporting infrastructure, and shipped features on quarterly cycles. The work was solid and the team was great. But the problems were bounded. You knew what "done" looked like before you started. In graphics research, particularly in neural rendering, the problems feel genuinely open. We're still figuring out the right representations for 3D scenes, the right loss functions, the right tradeoffs between quality and efficiency.

The transition wasn't smooth. The first semester of the PhD was humbling — reading papers took forever, my math was rusty, and I missed the immediate feedback loop of shipping code. But somewhere around the second year, something clicked. The freedom to chase a question wherever it leads, to build something nobody has built before, to be wrong for months and then suddenly right — that's what I was missing. I don't romanticize academia and I don't regret industry. They're different modes of working, and I needed both.
